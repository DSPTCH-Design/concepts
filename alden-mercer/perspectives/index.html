I. Featured · Disputes & strategy

When a business dispute becomes a strategic decision

A dispute can influence far more than a legal position. Commercial relationships, leadership attention and long-term priorities all belong in the conversation.

Most disputes arrive framed as a legal question: who is right, what the contract says, what a court is likely to do. Those questions matter. But they are rarely the only ones that determine whether the outcome is a good one for the business.

A dispute with a long-standing supplier carries a different weight than one with a former competitor. A disagreement among owners affects morale, hiring and the confidence of lenders long before anyone files anything. The cost of a dispute includes the hours leadership spends on it and the opportunities set aside while it runs.

That is why the first conversation about a dispute should cover the business as much as the law. What does a good result look like in a year? Which relationships need to survive it? How much uncertainty can the company carry, and for how long?

The answers often change the strategy. Sometimes they point toward a firm, early position. Sometimes they favor a negotiated path that preserves something more valuable than the immediate claim. Either way, the decision is made with the full picture in view.

II. Early counsel

The value of counsel before the turning point

Early conversations can bring clarity to contracts, partnerships and important business decisions.

Businesses tend to call a lawyer when something has already gone wrong. By then, the options are narrower and the decisions more expensive.

The more useful moment is earlier: before a partnership is signed, before a key hire receives equity, before a growth plan depends on a contract no one has read closely in years. A short conversation at that point can surface the questions worth answering while there is still time to answer them well.

Early counsel is less about drafting and more about perspective. It helps leaders see where an agreement allocates risk, which commitments are hard to undo, and what to watch for as the business changes.

III. Risk

What thoughtful risk management looks like in practice

A practical framework for identifying the questions worth asking before commitments become complicated.

Risk management is often treated as a checklist. In practice it works better as a habit of asking a few consistent questions before a commitment is made.

What happens if this goes well, and what happens if it does not? Who carries the cost of a delay? Which promises are we making that we cannot easily take back? Where does this agreement depend on people or circumstances that may change?

None of these questions requires a long memo. Asked early and answered honestly, they make it easier to negotiate the terms that matter and to let go of the ones that do not.
